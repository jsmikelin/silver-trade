Shipping Silver Safely: Bullion Transport Insurance and Security for B2B Buyers

📅 August 19, 2026📂 Transport & Insurance Guide⏱ 12 min read

Silver is one of the most value-dense commodities in the physical supply chain. A single 10 kg carton of 999 fine bars is worth roughly US$11,000–13,000 at mid-2026 prices, and a 20-foot container loaded with 15,000 kg of Good Delivery bars can exceed US$17 million. Move that value across borders and you inherit a specific set of risks: theft and internal collusion, damage and mysterious disappearance, customs holds and seizure, and — on some corridors — war and political exposure. For B2B buyers, the difference between a well-protected shipment and a badly protected one is measured in six figures. This guide explains how professional buyers structure transit insurance, security, and contract terms for silver bullion shipments, based on how HK Changjiang International Limited ships silver from Hong Kong to buyers across the United States, Japan, Europe, and the Middle East.

The Risk Profile of a Bullion Shipment

Before choosing insurance and security measures, it helps to map exactly what can go wrong between the seller's warehouse and your receiving dock. Precious metals insurers classify bullion risks into four families.

Theft — including internal theft

Theft is the dominant risk for silver in transit because of its value density: a shoebox-sized parcel of bars can carry a five-figure value, and a single pallet can carry seven figures. Every touchpoint is an exposure — the seller's warehouse, courier depots, air cargo terminals, port facilities, transshipment hubs, and the destination warehouse. Internal theft is the more insidious variant: warehouse staff, drivers, or customs agents who know the schedule and the contents can stage losses, substitute counterfeit bars, or collaborate with external crews. That is why insurers impose security warranties (dual custody, CCTV, tamper-evident seals) and why the burden of proof in a claim sits with the assured — if you cannot demonstrate the loss happened during the insured transit and that warranties were met, the claim can be declined. Insurers look very carefully at claims involving internal staff, which is why documented chain-of-custody records are your best protection.

Damage and loss in transit

Physical damage and mysterious disappearance account for a smaller but persistent share of claims: fork-lift punctures of outer cartons, crushed boxes under heavy freight, container condensation and moisture damage, packages dropped or lost during transshipment, and in rare sea-transit cases, cargo jettisoned or washed overboard in heavy weather. Bar surfaces can be dented, grain bags can tear, and powder can be contaminated — all of which erode the melt value or the merchantability of the shipment.

Customs seizure and regulatory holds

Silver attracts customs and anti-money-laundering scrutiny at both ends of the journey. Misdeclared values, missing assay certificates, inconsistent documentation, or sanctions-related red flags can trigger holds, fines, or outright seizure. Crucially, seizure driven by the assured's own misdeclaration is generally uninsurable — standard cargo policies do not respond to the assured's illegal or fraudulent acts. The only reliable protection is accurate, complete documentation from the outset: correct HS code (7106.91 for unwrought silver), true weight, true value, and clean chain-of-title.

War and political risk

Standard cargo insurance excludes war, strikes, riots, and civil commotion unless separate extensions are purchased — the Institute War Clauses (Cargo) for sea shipments, the Institute War Clauses (Air Cargo) for air, and the Institute Strikes Clauses (Cargo). For routes passing through or near conflict zones, or destinations with unstable governments, buyers should also consider political risk elements such as export controls, sanctions, and restrictions on transferring payment. These are available from specialist political risk insurers and are priced per route.

How Bullion Transit Insurance Works

The global standard for cargo insurance is the market clause system developed in London and used by insurers worldwide. Silver shipments are typically covered under one of two structures: a marine cargo policy written on the Institute Cargo Clauses, or a specialist Valuable Items Transit (VIT) policy for precious metals.

Institute Cargo Clauses A, B and C

The Institute Cargo Clauses (originally drafted by the Institute of London Underwriters, now maintained by the Lloyd's Market Association and the International Underwriting Association) define three levels of cover. For bullion, the choice is effectively between A and nothing — the narrower clauses do not respond to theft.

ClauseCoverageSuitability for silver
ICC (A)"All risks" — covers all loss or damage except the standard exclusions (packing defects, inherent vice, delay, war/strikes, seizure, willful misconduct, etc.). Includes theft and piracy.Default choice for bullion. Theft is covered.
ICC (B)Named perils only: fire/explosion; stranding, sinking or capsizing; overturning or derailment of land conveyance; collision; discharge at a port of distress; earthquake, volcanic eruption or lightning; general average sacrifice; jettison; washing overboard; entry of sea, lake or river water; total loss of a package lost overboard or dropped during loading/unloading.Not suitable. Theft is NOT covered.
ICC (C)Narrowest named-peril cover: fire/explosion; stranding, sinking or capsizing; overturning or derailment; collision; general average sacrifice; jettison.Not suitable for bullion. This is the legal minimum under CIF — and precisely why you must not accept the default CIF cover for silver.

Valuable Items Transit (VIT) and All-Risk cover

For precious metals, most professional shipments are insured under specialist "Valuable Items Transit" policies — an all-risks wording designed for high-value cargo, often written on an agreed-value basis. Unlike a standard replacement-cost policy, the sum insured is fixed at declaration, so there is no argument about the value of the cargo at the time of loss. Typical VIT cover includes theft and robbery, mysterious disappearance (subject to proof), physical damage, and temporary storage at named depots within the transit. Insurers attach conditions appropriate to the risk: approved carriers, armored transport above certain thresholds, advance notice of high-value consignments, and dual custody at handover.

Warehouse-to-warehouse cover

Standard cargo transit clauses attach from the seller's warehouse or place of storage and terminate on delivery to the buyer's final warehouse — this is the warehouse-to-warehouse (W/W) principle. For sea transit, cover continues for up to 60 days after discharge at the port of destination, whichever happens first. Two practical consequences: first, once the goods are delivered to your warehouse, transit cover stops, and you need separate storage insurance; second, if a shipment is warehoused for longer than the policy allows during transit, notify the insurer in advance to extend the cover.

The Lloyd's market and how bullion risks are placed

Bullion transit is a specialist risk historically written in the Lloyd's of London market, alongside the London company market. Lloyd's syndicates have deep experience with precious metals claims — including theft and mysterious disappearance — and can provide agreed-value cover with the security warranties that other markets are unwilling to offer. Premiums for bullion transit typically range from 0.08–0.15% of declared value for air freight to 0.12–0.25% for sea freight from Hong Kong, depending on route, packaging, and carrier.

Insured value = melt value

Bullion is a commodity, so the insurable interest is the metal's melt value: weight × purity × spot price — not a "replacement cost" as with manufactured goods. Claims settle at melt value at the time of loss, and policies are typically written as agreed value at declaration. Because the silver price can move several percent during a 3–5 week sea transit, professional buyers insure at declared value plus a margin — commonly 110% of the CIF value — so a price rise in transit does not leave them under-insured. Under-insurance is penalised by the "average" condition, which scales down every claim by the ratio of insured value to true value.

What Is Covered — and What Is Not

On an all-risks VIT or ICC (A) policy, the covered perils include theft and robbery, mysterious disappearance (with proof), physical damage from transit accidents, general average contributions, and sue-and-labour expenses incurred to prevent a loss. The exclusions matter just as much, and these are the ones that cause the most rejected claims:

  • Insufficient or unsuitable packing. If the outer packaging does not meet the insurer's warranty — tamper-evident, crush-resistant, unmarked — cover can be voided entirely. Packing defects are the single most common reason bullion claims are declined.
  • Under-declared or undeclared value. Declaring less than the true value to save on premium or customs duty triggers the average condition on claims, and a fraudulent customs declaration can void the whole policy and invite seizure.
  • Delay, loss of market, and price fluctuation. Not covered. Price hedging during long transits is a treasury decision, not an insurance one.
  • War, strikes, riots, and civil commotion. Excluded unless the Institute War Clauses and Institute Strikes Clauses extensions are purchased separately.
  • Seizure, confiscation, arrest, restraint or detainment by governments or customs authorities. Misdeclaration-driven seizure is uninsurable in practice.
  • Willful misconduct or fraud of the assured. Standard exclusion in every policy.
  • Internal theft and collusion. All-risks wordings can respond, but you must prove the loss occurred during insured transit and that security warranties were met. Chain-of-custody logs, dual custody records, and seal numbers are your evidence.

Security Best Practices for B2B Shipments

Insurance transfers the financial risk of a loss; security reduces the probability of one. The two work together, because insurers price and sometimes condition cover on the security measures you actually use.

Armored transport for the highest-value legs

For high-value bullion, the road legs are the most vulnerable part of the journey. Specialist carriers — Brink's, Loomis, and Malca-Amit are the best-known names in precious-metals logistics — move high-value cargo in armored vehicles, often with armed escort, and are the standard for bank-to-bank bullion transfers. For commercial shipments, insurers frequently warrant armored transport on the origin and destination legs (warehouse to airport, port to final delivery), with standard security couriers acceptable for the middle legs. Malca-Amit in particular has built a business on secure precious-metals air freight with dedicated handling at major airports.

Unmarked, tamper-evident packaging

Outer packaging should reveal nothing about its contents: neutral cartons or wooden crates, no logos, no "precious metals" wording, no bar counts. Transport documents should carry generic commodity descriptions such as "metal products" on air waybills and bills of lading — while the customs declaration, which is a separate legal document, must always state the true description (silver, HS 7106.91), weight, and value. Tamper-evident seals with recorded numbers on every package, photographed before dispatch, give you both deterrence and proof. HK Changjiang photographs all seals and packages before every dispatch.

Split shipments to cap single-loss exposure

For large consignments, splitting the value across multiple shipments — different flights, different days, different routes — means no single event can destroy the whole consignment. Insurers may require this above certain value thresholds, and it is standard practice for institutional buyers shipping above US$1 million in one month.

End-to-end tracking and chain of custody

Sea containers holding high-value silver should carry GPS trackers that alert the logistics team to route deviation or unexpected opening. Air shipments are tracked in real time per flight segment. At every handover — seller warehouse, carrier depot, airport/port, destination — custody should be documented with dual witnesses, signed receipts, and seal-number verification against the packing list.

Declared value strategy

Declare the true value to the insurer, the freight forwarder, and the customs authority. Under-declaring to reduce duty is a false economy: it triggers average penalties on insurance claims, voids cover if discovered, and is the most common cause of customs seizure. The "neutral description" practice applies only to transport documents, never to customs declarations.

Incoterms 2020: Who Bears the Risk, Who Insures

The Incoterms rule in your contract determines where risk transfers and therefore whose insurance must be in place. Under the current Incoterms 2020 edition, the four rules that matter most for silver shipments are:

IncotermRisk transfer pointWho arranges insurance
EXW (Ex Works)When goods are placed at the buyer's disposal at the seller's premises.Buyer arranges all transport and insurance from origin — your policy must attach at the seller's door.
FOB (Free On Board) — sea; FCA for air/any modeWhen goods are on board the vessel (FOB) or handed to the carrier (FCA).Buyer insures from the vessel/carrier handover; seller must notify shipment so the policy attaches correctly.
CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight)On board the vessel — the same point as FOB.Seller contracts freight and must insure — but only at minimum ICC (C) cover, 110% of CIF value, unless otherwise agreed. For bullion, never accept this default: negotiate ICC (A) or VIT all-risks plus war/strikes extensions.
DAP (Delivered at Place)When goods are placed at the buyer's disposal at the named destination place.Seller bears all transit risk but is NOT obliged to insure. If the truck is hijacked en route, the seller absorbs the loss — so the contract should state who insures and at what value.

B2B contract checklist for every silver purchase:

  • Specify the policy type (ICC (A) or VIT all-risks) and the named insured — you can insure both parties' interests on one policy.
  • Specify the insured value basis: declared value plus margin (110%+), with melt-value settlement language.
  • State who pays the premium, who files claims, and who is loss payee.
  • Define proof requirements: assay certificate, commercial invoice, packing list with weights and seal numbers, photographs.
  • Agree loss-reporting deadlines — typically 30 days from discovery — and the notification procedure for delay or deviation.

LBMA Responsible Sourcing and Supply Chain Traceability

Insurance and security protect the physical metal; responsible sourcing protects its marketability. The LBMA Responsible Sourcing Programme (RSP) requires all refiners on the LBMA Good Delivery List to undergo independent third-party audits against the OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Supply Chains of Minerals. The audit trail runs from the mine through the refiner to the trader: refinery certificates, source declarations, and chain-of-custody records must be complete and consistent.

For B2B buyers, this is not a formality. Banks, refiners, and large industrial end-users increasingly refuse metal without an RSP-compliant provenance trail, and the same records are what customs and AML regulators ask for when a high-value shipment is inspected. A shipment of silver with clean provenance clears faster, sells easier, and never triggers a sourcing investigation. Frequently Asked Questions

What does bullion transit insurance actually cover?

Under an all-risks VIT or ICC (A) policy: physical loss of or damage to the silver during the insured journey — theft, robbery, mysterious disappearance (subject to proof), and transit damage — from the seller's warehouse to the buyer's warehouse (warehouse-to-warehouse), including temporary storage within policy limits. Claims settle on melt value.

What does bullion transit insurance NOT cover?

Insufficient or unsuitable packing, under-declared value, delay and loss of market, war and strikes (without extensions), government seizure, willful misconduct, and internal theft where you cannot prove the loss and meet security warranties such as dual custody.

Air freight or sea freight — which is safer and cheaper to insure?

Air freight has a shorter exposure window and typically lower premium rates (0.08–0.15% of declared value versus 0.12–0.25% for sea freight), but costs far more per kilogram. Sea freight is economical for volume but extends the risk window by weeks. Our detailed air vs sea freight comparison covers the full trade-off.

How do I prove the value of my silver for insurance and claims?

Keep a third-party assay certificate, commercial invoice, packing list with exact weights and seal numbers, and photographs before dispatch. Bullion policies are agreed-value at declaration, and claims settle at melt value — weight × purity × spot price at the time of loss.

What is the Hong Kong export process for silver?

Hong Kong is a free port: no export duty and no export license for silver. You need a commercial invoice, packing list, assay certificate, and air waybill or bill of lading under HS 7106.91. Destination rules matter: the United States imports silver bullion duty-free with an accurate CBP entry (see our US silver import guide), while Japan applies a 10% consumption tax at import. More detail in our Hong Kong silver export guide.

If a truck is hijacked under a CIF contract, whose insurance pays?

Under CIF, risk transfers when the goods are on board the vessel and the seller's insurance runs for the buyer's account after that point. Under EXW or FOB the buyer bears the risk from an earlier point, so the buyer's own policy must attach accordingly. Always confirm the policy attachment matches the Incoterm.

Do I need LBMA-compliant supply even for industrial silver?

Increasingly yes. Refiners, banks, and large end-users require provenance compliant with the LBMA Responsible Sourcing Programme, audited against the OECD Due Diligence Guidance. Request the refiner's certificate and assay documentation on every purchase.

Conclusion

Safe silver shipping is a system, not a single measure: a correct insurance structure (all-risks VIT or ICC (A), agreed value at melt value plus margin, warehouse-to-warehouse), a security protocol that matches the value (armored transport, unmarked tamper-evident packaging, split shipments, full tracking), and a contract that assigns risk and insurance responsibility cleanly under the right Incoterm. Add LBMA-compliant provenance and accurate documentation, and the shipment is protected financially, physically, and commercially.
